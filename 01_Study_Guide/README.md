# PART I — NORMATIVE ETHICAL THEORIES

Normative ethics asks: *what ought we to do?* The three big families tested in PBIO 2026 are **Utilitarianism** (consequences), **Deontology** (duties), and **Virtue/Telos ethics** (character + purpose).

---

## 1. Utilitarianism (Bentham, J.S. Mill — via Sandel)

### Core Idea
The morally right action is the one that produces **the greatest balance of happiness over suffering** for the greatest number of people affected. Morality = maximizing aggregate welfare.

### Key Concepts
- **Consequentialism:** only outcomes matter; intentions and rules are instrumental.
- **Hedonic calculus (Bentham):** pleasures and pains can in principle be measured, summed, and compared on a single scale.
- **Principle of utility:** "It is the greatest happiness of the greatest number that is the measure of right and wrong."
- **Higher vs. lower pleasures (Mill):** Mill modifies Bentham — intellectual/moral pleasures are qualitatively superior to bodily ones. "Better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied."
- **Act vs. Rule utilitarianism:**
  - *Act:* judge each individual action by its consequences.
  - *Rule:* follow rules whose general adoption maximizes utility (a partial answer to deontological objections).

### Key Thinkers
- **Jeremy Bentham** — founder; *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789).
- **John Stuart Mill** — *Utilitarianism* (1863) refines the theory with the qualitative distinction between higher and lower pleasures; his *harm principle* (the only legitimate ground for coercing an adult is to prevent harm to others) belongs to *On Liberty* (1859) and is often used to *supplement* utilitarianism with a protection of individual liberty.

### Sandel's Critiques (memorize these — they appear in slides)
1. **Rights problem:** if torturing an innocent maximizes happiness, utilitarianism seems to permit it. Example Sandel uses: throwing Christians to lions delights the Roman crowd → utility says "do it," intuition says "monstrous."
2. **Single-currency problem:** can we really weigh all values (love, dignity, life, money) on one scale? Sandel cites Ford Pinto cost-benefit analyses and cigarette company calculations of "death savings" as morally repugnant exercises in utilitarian accounting.
3. **Distribution problem:** utilitarianism cares about totals, not how happiness is distributed — it can justify sacrificing minorities.

### Strengths
- Democratic and impartial (each person counts as one).
- Intuitive in policy: cost–benefit analysis, public health triage, vaccine distribution.
- Naturally extends to animals (Singer): if suffering is the bad, **all** suffering counts.

### Exam-style questions
- State the principle of utility and explain Mill's modification of Bentham.
- Give two of Sandel's objections to utilitarianism. How might a utilitarian reply?
- Apply act vs. rule utilitarianism to lying to save a friend.

## 2. Deontology / Kantian Ethics (Immanuel Kant — via Sandel)

### Core Idea
An action is right if it is done from **duty**, in accordance with a moral law that any rational being would will to be **universal**. Consequences and inclinations are morally irrelevant; what matters is the maxim (rule) behind your will.

### Key Concepts
- **Good will:** the only thing good without qualification (opening of *Groundwork*).
- **Duty vs. inclination:** acting morally = acting from duty, not from desire, self-interest, or even sympathy.
- **Autonomy vs. heteronomy:** moral law comes from our own rational will (autonomy), not from external authority, God, or desires (heteronomy).
- **Hypothetical vs. categorical imperatives:**
  - *Hypothetical:* "If you want X, do Y" — instrumental.
  - *Categorical:* unconditional — "Do Y, period."
- **Three formulations of the Categorical Imperative:**
  1. **Universal Law:** "Act only on that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law."
  2. **Humanity as End:** "Act so as to treat humanity, whether in your own person or in another, always as an end and never merely as a means."
  3. **Kingdom of Ends:** act as if you were a legislating member of a community of rational beings each treating the others as ends.
- **Dignity (Würde):** persons have *dignity*, not *price*. Things have price (replaceable); persons are irreplaceable.

### Sandel's Worked Examples
- **Lying:** even lying to a murderer at the door is, on Kant's strict view, impermissible — universalizing lying destroys the practice of promising/communication.
- **Suicide / self-respect:** treats one's own person merely as a means to escape pain.
- **False promises, neglect of talents, refusing to help others:** all fail the universalizability or humanity test.

### Strengths
- Anchors **human rights** (you can't trade dignity for utility).
- Strong account of why some actions (torture, slavery, deception) are wrong *in themselves*.

### Standard Objections
- **Rigorism:** can lead to monstrous results (truth-telling to murderer).
- **Conflict of duties:** Kant gives no clear procedure when duties clash.
- **Empty formalism (Hegel):** the universalizability test is too thin to generate concrete duties on its own.

### Exam-style questions
- State the two main formulations of the Categorical Imperative and apply each to making a lying promise.
- What does Kant mean by *autonomy* and why is it the basis of dignity?
- Compare Kant's and Mill's verdict on torturing one to save many.

## 3. Aristotle: Virtue Ethics & Telos (via Sandel)

### Core Idea
Justice and morality are about **fitting persons to their proper roles and virtues** in light of the **telos** (purpose, end, function) of the practice in question. The moral question is: *what kind of person should I become?* — not just *what should I do?*.

### Key Concepts
- **Telos:** every practice/object/role has an end or purpose. To reason about justice we must first ask *what is this for?*.
- **Virtue (aretē):** an excellence of character; a disposition to feel and act well. Virtues are acquired by **habituation**, not just instruction.
- **Phronesis (practical wisdom):** the ability to perceive what the situation morally requires; cannot be reduced to rules.
- **Eudaimonia:** flourishing, the highest human good — living a life that fulfills our rational/social telos.
- **Doctrine of the mean:** virtue lies between two vices (e.g., courage between cowardice and recklessness).
- **Justice as honorific (Sandel):** distributive questions ("who should get the best flutes?") are answered by asking the *purpose* of flutes — to be played well — and the *virtues* the practice should honor (musical excellence). Therefore the best flutes go to the best flute players.

### Sandel's Examples
- **The flute case** (Aristotle): best flutes → best flute-players. The good distributed *and* the people honored both follow from telos.
- **Golf carts (Casey Martin case):** is the *telos* of pro golf walking the course, or executing shots? Sandel uses this as a modern teleological dispute.
- **Cheerleading / political offices:** Aristotle thinks political office should go to those most able to deliberate well about the common good, because that is the *telos* of politics.

### Strengths
- Captures moral psychology: character, not just isolated decisions.
- Makes sense of moral education and role models.
- Explains why we honor certain practices (medicine, teaching, parenthood) differently.

### Objections
- Risks **conservatism** — fixing teloi may entrench traditional roles (Sandel acknowledges this).
- Disagreement about telos (the cart case).
- Less guidance for novel cases (bioethics, AI).

### Exam-style questions
- Define telos and explain Aristotle's "flute argument."
- What is phronesis and why can't it be replaced by rules?
- Where does Sandel see virtue ethics succeeding where utilitarianism and Kantianism fail?

## Quick Comparison Table

| Aspect | Utilitarianism | Deontology (Kant) | Virtue / Telos (Aristotle) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Right action | Maximizes net happiness | Conforms to universalizable duty | Expresses virtuous character; fits telos |
| Focus | Consequences | Will / intention / rules | Character + purpose |
| Moral subject | Sentient beings | Rational agents (persons) | Members of a community/practice |
| Slogan | "Greatest happiness" | "Treat as end, never merely as means" | "Become the kind of person who…" |
| Strength | Impartial, scalable | Protects dignity & rights | Realistic moral psychology |
| Weakness | May violate rights | Rigid; conflict of duties | Disputed teloi; conservative |

# PART II — DESCRIPTIVE ETHICS (Chudárková)

### Core Idea
Descriptive ethics studies *how people actually make moral judgments* — empirically, using psychology, neuroscience, anthropology — without prescribing what they *should* do. It is the **is** to normative ethics' **ought** (Hume's distinction).

### Key Concepts
- **Moral intuitions:** rapid, automatic, often emotional evaluative reactions that *precede* explicit reasoning.
- **Dual-process model:** System 1 (fast, intuitive, emotional) vs. System 2 (slow, deliberate, reasoned). Most everyday moral judgment is System 1.
- **Social Intuitionist Model (Jonathan Haidt):** moral judgment first, justification second — reasoning is largely *post-hoc* rationalization. Haidt's metaphor: *"the emotional dog and its rational tail"* — the dog (intuition) wags the tail (reasoning), not the other way round. Reasoning still matters **socially**: it is how we persuade others and how groups revise norms over time.
- **Moral Foundations Theory (Haidt, Graham, Joseph):** originally **five** recurrent foundations across cultures — Care/Harm, Fairness/Cheating, Loyalty/Betrayal, Authority/Subversion, Sanctity/Degradation — with **Liberty/Oppression** later proposed as a candidate sixth foundation.
- **Trolley problem & neuroethics (Joshua Greene):** *personal* ("up-close") dilemmas — e.g., pushing the large man off a footbridge — recruit emotional/affective brain regions (vmPFC, amygdala) and tend to elicit characteristically *deontological* judgments; *impersonal* dilemmas — e.g., flipping a switch — recruit cognitive-control regions (DLPFC) and tend to elicit *utilitarian* judgments. Greene reads this as empirical support for a **dual-process** picture of moral cognition.
- **Cultural and developmental variation:** Kohlberg's stages of moral development; Carol Gilligan's *ethic of care* critique; cross-cultural studies showing that much psychological data comes from **WEIRD** populations (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) and may not generalize.
- **Naturalistic fallacy / is–ought gap:** descriptive findings *cannot by themselves* settle normative questions, but they constrain plausible normative theories.

### Why descriptive ethics matters for bioethics
- It shows that "common moral sense" is variable, biased, and emotion-driven — important when designing informed-consent procedures, animal-welfare regulations, or research-ethics committees.
- It helps explain why some ethical issues (e.g., gene editing, animal testing) trigger strong gut reactions even when consequentialist analysis is favorable.

### Exam-style questions
- Distinguish descriptive and normative ethics. Give one example of each.
- Explain Haidt's Social Intuitionist Model. What evidence supports it?
- How can neuroimaging studies of the trolley problem inform debates between utilitarians and deontologists?

# PART III — ANIMAL TESTING & THE 3R PRINCIPLES

Sources: NanoEt expert system (3R principles); Soucitně.cz article (Vandrovcová) summarizing pro/contra arguments; PBIO 2026 lecture notes.

## 3.1 The ethical question
Is it morally permissible to use non-human animals — beings capable of suffering — as means in biomedical research aimed at benefiting humans (and other animals)?

## 3.2 Arguments PRO animal testing

### A. Human priority / membership in the moral community
- **Carl Cohen:** rights are essentially human; they require participation in a community of moral agents able to recognize duties. Animals can't, so they have no rights — though we still have *duties of humane treatment* toward them.
- **Patrick Bateson:** rights are a *consensual contract*; assigning rights to animals is as nonsensical as giving them the vote.
- **Alex Epstein:** extending rights to animals would prevent life-saving research and amount to "human extinction."
- **Josie Appleton:** humans are uniquely *self-aware*, *reflective*, and *self-improving* — a difference of kind, not just degree.
- **Ilana Mercer:** animals "answer the call of the wild" — they are not moral agents, do not have duties, and so cannot have rights.

### B. Medical and scientific necessity
- Vaccines (polio, COVID-19), insulin, anesthesia, transplant medicine — historically depended on animal models.
- Some biological complexity (immune response, whole-organ behavior, multigenerational effects) is not yet fully replicable *in silico* or *in vitro*.

### C. Theological / human-exceptionalism arguments
- Humans bear the *imago Dei* and possess immortal souls; animals were given for human use (Aquinas).

## 3.3 Arguments CONTRA animal testing

### A. Sentience-based (utilitarian)
- **Peter Singer (*Animal Liberation*, 1975):** the morally relevant property is **sentience** — the capacity to suffer. Ignoring an animal's suffering because of species is **speciesism**, structurally analogous to racism or sexism.
- The utilitarian calculus must include animal suffering on equal terms with human suffering of the same intensity.

### B. Rights-based (deontological)
- **Tom Regan (*The Case for Animal Rights*, 1983):** mammals over ~1 year are **subjects-of-a-life** — they have beliefs, desires, memory, a sense of the future, an experiential welfare. They therefore have **inherent value** and cannot be used merely as means. The only consistent conclusion is the **abolition** of animal research.
- **David Thomas:** if we don't experiment on humans without consent because they cannot consent, we should not experiment on animals either — they cannot consent and they suffer.

### C. Marginal cases / consistency
- Cognitive criteria (rationality, language, autonomy) exclude infants, severely cognitively disabled humans, and Alzheimer patients. We don't experiment on them. So cognition cannot be the real criterion — what is doing the work is **species membership**, i.e. speciesism (Ball & Norris).

### D. Scientific objections
- **Translational failure:** ~90% of drugs that succeed in animal trials fail in human trials (toxicity or lack of efficacy).
- **Species differences** in metabolism, immune systems, microbiome.
- **Reproducibility crisis** in animal studies (housing, stress, sex bias).

### E. Welfare and dignity
- Even "humane" laboratory housing causes chronic stress in social, intelligent species.
- The Helsinki Declaration on human research stresses the welfare of the individual subject — a principle inconsistently applied to animals.

## 3.4 The 3R Principles (Russell & Burch, 1959)
The current legal and ethical framework (EU Directive 2010/63/EU, Czech law) is built on the **3Rs**:

1. **Replacement** — use non-animal alternatives whenever possible:
   - *Absolute* replacement: cell cultures, organoids, organ-on-chip, *in silico* models, computational toxicology, human volunteer studies, microdosing.
   - *Relative* replacement: invertebrates, early embryos, ex vivo tissues from animals killed humanely for other reasons.
2. **Reduction** — use the minimum number of animals consistent with valid statistical conclusions:
   - Better experimental design, power analysis, sharing of data and tissues, systematic reviews to avoid duplication.
3. **Refinement** — minimize pain, distress, lasting harm; enhance welfare:
   - Anesthesia/analgesia, humane endpoints, environmental enrichment, training animals to cooperate, non-invasive imaging instead of terminal procedures.

> Mnemonic: **R**eplace if you can, **R**educe if you must, **R**efine what remains.

### Regulatory layer
- **EU Directive 2010/63/EU**: ethics review, project authorization, severity classification (non-recovery / mild / moderate / severe), retrospective assessment, ban on great-ape research (with narrow exceptions).
- **Czech law:** Act 246/1992 Sb. on the protection of animals against cruelty (as amended).

### Exam-style questions
- Define each of the 3Rs and give one concrete example of each.
- Compare Singer's and Regan's positions: what does each take to be the morally relevant property?
- What is speciesism, and how do critics argue from "marginal cases"?
- Cohen says rights require a moral community. How do Ball & Norris reply?

# PART IV — OPEN SCIENCE & OPEN DATA

Sources: Ross-Hellauer et al., *Dynamics of cumulative advantage and threats to equity in open science*; LSE Impact, *It's time to extend the FAIR principles*; PBIO 2026 lecture.

## 4.1 What is Open Science?
Open Science = making the **whole research process** — questions, methods, data, code, publications, peer review, education — transparent, accessible, reusable, and participatory.

### Pillars
- **Open Access (OA)** to publications (Gold, Green, Diamond routes).
- **Open Data** (raw + processed).
- **Open Source / Open Code** (analysis scripts, software).
- **Open Methodology / preregistration** (e.g., OSF).
- **Open Peer Review.**
- **Citizen Science / Open Engagement.**
- **Open Educational Resources (OER).**

### Why?
- Faster, more reliable science (reproducibility crisis).
- Public money → public results.
- Equitable access for low/middle-income countries and the public.
- Enables AI/meta-analysis at scale.

## 4.2 FAIR Principles (Wilkinson et al., 2016)
Data should be:
- **F — Findable:** unique persistent identifiers (DOIs), rich metadata, indexed in searchable repositories.
- **A — Accessible:** retrievable by their identifier using a standardized, open protocol; metadata remain accessible even if data are restricted.
- **I — Interoperable:** use formal, shared, broadly applicable vocabularies/ontologies; standard formats.
- **R — Reusable:** rich provenance, clear license (e.g., CC-BY, CC0), domain-relevant community standards.

> FAIR ≠ "Open." Data can be FAIR yet access-controlled (e.g., sensitive medical data with controlled access).

## 4.3 CARE Principles (Indigenous Data Governance — Carroll et al., 2020)
Complement FAIR by centering people and purpose, especially Indigenous communities:
- **C — Collective benefit**
- **A — Authority to control**
- **R — Responsibility**
- **E — Ethics**

## 4.4 LSE Impact extension — *beyond* FAIR
Calls for FAIR to be coupled with values such as:
- **Equity** (don't entrench existing advantages),
- **Inclusivity** (multilingual metadata, accessible repositories),
- **Sustainability** (long-term funding for repositories),
- **Provenance & accountability** (who produced the data, under what conditions).

## 4.5 Ross-Hellauer — Cumulative Advantage & Equity
Open Science's open infrastructures can paradoxically **amplify inequalities** ("Matthew effect"):
- **APC barriers:** Gold OA charges price out under-resourced authors.
- **Visibility advantage:** already-prominent labs gain even more citations through OA.
- **Infrastructure asymmetry:** rich institutions can support data stewards, repositories, RDM staff.
- **Evaluation systems** still reward high-impact, English-language venues.

### Recommended mitigations
- Diamond OA (no fees for author or reader), institutional and national funding.
- Recognize and reward sharing in evaluation (DORA, CoARA).
- Support multilingualism, regional repositories, and capacity building.
- Apply CARE alongside FAIR.

### Exam-style questions
- What does FAIR stand for? Give one concrete way to make a dataset Interoperable.
- Why is FAIR not the same as Open?
- Explain *cumulative advantage* in Open Science. Give two policy responses.
- How do CARE principles complement FAIR?
