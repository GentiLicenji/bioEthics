## Section B — Short Answer

**16. Kant on a false promise.**
- *Universal Law:* the maxim "I will make a lying promise whenever convenient" cannot be universalized — if everyone did so, promises would lose their meaning and the very act of promising would become impossible. The maxim self-destructs.
- *Humanity:* a false promise uses the lender *merely as a means* (to get the money) — the lender cannot rationally consent to a deception, so their dignity is bypassed.
- Conclusion: lying promise is impermissible on both formulations.

**17. The 3Rs.**
- *Replacement:* using non-animal alternatives (e.g., human reconstructed cornea like EpiOcular instead of the Draize rabbit test).
- *Reduction:* using fewer animals (e.g., proper power analysis, sharing tissues across labs).
- *Refinement:* reducing pain/distress (e.g., analgesia, environmental enrichment, humane endpoints, non-invasive imaging).

**18. The flute argument.** To answer "who should get the best flutes?" Aristotle says we must first ask the *telos* of flutes — to be played excellently. Therefore the best flutes go to the best flute players. This shows that distributive justice is *teleological* (depends on purpose) and *honorific* (it expresses what the practice celebrates), unlike utilitarian "maximize satisfaction" or pure-procedural answers.

**19. Descriptive vs. normative.**
- *Descriptive* describes how people *do* judge; *normative* prescribes how they *should*.
- Hume's gap means we can't derive *ought* from *is*. But descriptive findings are still relevant: e.g., Greene's fMRI showing that deontological judgments correlate with affective responses can challenge the *evidential weight* of intuitions about up-close harm, motivating utilitarians to discount them. This doesn't *settle* the debate but constrains what counts as a defensible intuition.

**20. FAIR.** Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable. FAIR ≠ Open: "Accessible" means *retrievable via a standard protocol after authorization*, not necessarily public. Example: hospital genomic data can have a DOI, rich metadata, and a controlled-access procedure (FAIR), yet require an ethics-board approved data-access agreement (not Open).

## Section C — Essay (rubric)

A strong essay (E1–E3) typically:
- States a clear thesis up front.
- Defines key terms (utilitarianism / Kant / Aristotle / FAIR / 3R as relevant).
- Engages at least **two** authors per side.
- Anticipates one strong objection and replies to it.
- Reaches a qualified conclusion (not just "it depends").

For **E1 (utilitarianism + animals):** mention Singer (sentience), Regan (rights-based critique that utilitarianism lets too much through), Cohen (defense of human priority), and the 3Rs as a *practically utilitarian-leaning* compromise: it accepts animal use in principle but minimizes suffering and number, consistent with Singer but not satisfying Regan's abolitionism.

For **E2 (Open Science equity):** explain Matthew effect, APC barriers, infrastructure asymmetry; propose Diamond OA + reform of evaluation (DORA/CoARA); link to CARE for vulnerable groups.

For **E3 (transplant dilemma):**
- Utilitarian: 5 > 1 → maybe permit. But rule-utilitarianism would forbid (rule "kill patients for organs" wrecks medicine's trust).
- Kantian: clearly impermissible — using the patient *merely as a means*; violates dignity.
- Aristotelian: incompatible with the *telos* of medicine (healing) and the virtues of a physician (trustworthiness, *primum non nocere*).
- Most students defend Kant or Aristotle; explain why utilitarian intuition fails the rights/practice test.

## Section B — Case Analyses

### Case 1 — Cosmetics
**a) 3R mapping.**
- Doing the Draize test at all violates *Replacement* (validated in vitro alternatives like EpiOcular and BCOP exist).
- "Minimum number of rabbits" gestures at *Reduction*.
- Nothing in the proposal addresses *Refinement* (Draize is severe by design — no anesthesia of the cornea, prolonged exposure).

**b) Utilitarian vs. Kantian.**
- *Utilitarian:* aggregate suffering of rabbits + reputational harm vs. economic and consumer benefits; given that validated alternatives exist, the marginal scientific value is near zero, so utility likely opposes the test. A short-term utilitarian arguing for market access is on weak ground.
- *Kantian:* the rabbits are not persons in Kant's strict sense, but the firm is treating regulators and EU consumers as means by exploiting jurisdictional arbitrage to evade EU norms. This is also a violation of professional integrity (a duty to oneself).

**c) Virtue/phronesis.** A virtuous R&D director would not outsource cruelty to evade the spirit of the law; she would invest in validated alternatives, lobby the foreign regulator, and decline the market if necessary — embodying integrity, justice, and *phronesis* (perceiving that complying with the letter while violating the spirit corrupts the practice).

### Case 2 — Indigenous Genomics
**a) CC0 vs. CARE.** CC0 hands away all rights, removing the community's *Authority to control* secondary uses, undermining *Collective benefit* and *Responsibility*. So CC0 is **not** compatible with CARE.

**b) FAIR + CARE model.**
- Deposit data in a controlled-access repository (e.g., EGA) with a DOI and rich metadata (FAIR).
- Use a Traditional Knowledge / BC label or a custom license requiring re-consent for commercial or ancestry uses.
- Establish a community data-governance board with veto power over downstream projects.
- Provide capacity-building (training community members) for collective benefit.

**c) Cumulative advantage.** Well-resourced labs gain reuse value from the data, while the source community bears risk and rarely shares in citations, grants, or commercial returns — a textbook Matthew effect mediated by infrastructure asymmetry.

### Case 3 — Pandemic Trolley
**a)**
- *Utilitarian for (i):* expected lives saved by 18-month earlier deployment (≈1.8M+) likely dwarfs deaths from a poorly-tested vaccine, especially with adaptive monitoring.
- *Kantian objection:* deploying without consent / adequate evidence treats every recipient *merely as a means* in a public-health calculation; informed consent (autonomy) is non-negotiable.

**b) Helsinki Declaration.** Article 8 (and successors) places the welfare of the individual subject above the interest of science and society; this rules out (i) and constrains (ii) to genuine informed consent, independent ethics review, risk-minimization, and post-trial care.

**c) Descriptive ethics.** Haidt: public will react with strong intuitive aversion to "experimenting on people" (sanctity/harm foundations) even where utilitarian numbers favor it. Greene: option (i), being an *active* harm to identifiable persons, will trigger emotional/deontological brain responses; option (iii), passive (delay), feels safer despite higher death toll — the *omission bias*. Regulators must anticipate and communicate around these intuitions, not just argue from numbers.

## Section C — Essay (rubric)

**Part 1 (Singer vs. Regan and the 3Rs).**
- Singer's utilitarianism justifies graded use: replace where alternatives exist (more suffering avoided than benefits lost), reduce, refine — directly mappable to the 3Rs.
- Regan's rights view forbids any use in principle; the 3Rs at best slow the violation but do not justify it.
- Hence the EU framework is best read as **Singer-compatible** (and explicitly utilitarian in its harm-benefit analyses), not Regan-compatible.
- A nuanced answer notes that 3R *Replacement* is the bridge: it is utilitarian in form but rights-protective in effect.

**Part 2 (Sandel and honor).**
- Bentham measures only outcomes; Kant measures only universalizability/dignity. Neither asks *what is this practice for and what virtues should it celebrate?*
- Example — *Medicine:* its telos is healing; this explains why physician-assisted commercial drug-pushing or pay-for-organs schemes feel corrupt even if they "work" or are mutually consensual. Aristotle/Sandel can articulate the corruption-of-practice intuition that the other theories struggle with.
- A challenger could argue that "telos" is contested (Casey Martin) and risks conservatism — students should engage this objection.

## Bonus
- *Phronesis:* practical wisdom; the virtue of perceiving what a situation morally requires.
- *Speciesism:* unjustified moral discrimination based on species membership (Singer).
- *Heteronomy:* the will determined by something other than its own rational law (e.g., desire, authority); opposite of autonomy.
- *Cumulative advantage:* the tendency for early or structural advantages to compound (Matthew effect), e.g., in citations or funding.
- *Humane endpoint:* a predefined criterion to terminate an experiment / euthanize an animal before suffering becomes severe.
